# To The Moon editorial and teaching style

This is the house style for all player-facing copy in To The Moon. It uses AP style as a clarity and consistency baseline, then adapts it for a strategy game.

## Name the game clearly

- Call the product **To The Moon**.
- Use **this run**, **this mode**, **this account** or **the game** when one of those nouns is more precise.
- Reserve **the PFM Media Buying Trainer** for the formal subtitle and metadata. Do not call the game “the trainer” in instructions or definitions.

## Write for the player

- Write to **you**. Use active voice and concrete verbs.
- Lead with the goal, action or result. Avoid internal product language such as “evidence surface,” “deterministic action coach” or “operational state.”
- Use sentence case for headings, controls and labels. Mode titles and proper names may keep title capitalization.
- Prefer short sentences. Introduce one unfamiliar idea at a time.
- Use American English: **color**, **optimized**, **center**, **analyze**.
- Spell out one through nine in narrative copy. Use figures for 10 and above, and always use figures for game metrics, money, percentages, scores, dates and days: **Day 3**, **18%**, **$20,000**, **2.4x**.
- Spell out an unfamiliar abbreviation on first use: **marketing efficiency ratio (MER)**. Compact metric cards may use the abbreviation after that.
- Use an em dash with a space on both sides when it improves clarity. Use arrows, multiplication signs and dots only in compact data displays, not as tutorial grammar.
- Be specific. Replace vague terms such as “incident,” “execute,” “optimize” or “operate” with the actual event or action whenever space allows.

## Explain every mechanic

The player must be able to answer these questions before a mechanic affects a decision:

1. What is it?
2. Why does it matter now?
3. What changes it?
4. What can I do about it?
5. Where will I see the result?

Put essential consequences beside the control. A glossary is supporting help, not a substitute for a clear win condition, first move, metric headline or irreversible-action warning.

## Use the walkthrough rhythm

Every teaching sequence should follow this order:

1. **Orient:** Say where the player is and what the immediate goal is.
2. **Act:** Name one visible control and ask for one action.
3. **Observe:** Tell the player what changed on screen.
4. **Explain:** Connect the result to the media-buying idea.
5. **Continue:** Give the next action or a clear troubleshooting choice.

Tutorials should be interactive, optional and replayable. Early steps verify one action at a time. Later steps should assume only concepts the player has demonstrated, not concepts that appeared once in a paragraph.

## Label controls by consequence

Prefer **verb + object + immediate result or cost**:

- “Create one variation · $600” instead of “Multiply.”
- “Stop this ad · frees its daily allocation” instead of “Kill slot.”
- “Run Day 3” with “Spend selected budgets and reveal today’s results” instead of “Execute media buy.”
- “Start a new run with these settings” instead of “Apply and restart.”

## Coach without blaming

Debriefs use this order: observation, consequence, next attempt.

- “You changed the budget after one weak day. Daily results are noisy, so the change may have interrupted a strong ad. On the replay, wait for a three-day pattern before scaling or cutting it.”
